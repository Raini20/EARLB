\section{Introduction}
Autonomous robots on construction sites have to localise themselves in environments that
offer little of what SLAM systems rely on: corridors with repeating geometry, rooms behind
doors that open and close, staircases, and surfaces that are either featureless or highly
reflective. Accuracy requirements there are set by the task, not by the algorithm --- as-built
documentation and layout work are specified in centimetres, so a drift of a few decimetres
over a single storey already makes a map unusable. Which sensor modality delivers that
accuracy is not obvious from the literature, because published results are rarely obtained
on the same data with the same metrics.

Comparisons across modalities are hard for a mundane reason: the systems do not agree on what
they output. A visual-inertial system reports the camera or IMU pose, a LiDAR system the
sensor pose, at different rates, in different frames, with different time bases. Differences
introduced by that bookkeeping can easily exceed the differences between the algorithms.

This work therefore compares four SLAM systems of three modalities --- OpenMAVIS
(visual-inertial), MOLA-LO (LiDAR odometry), KISS-SLAM (LiDAR) and SR-LIVO
(LiDAR-inertial-visual) --- on one and the same sequence, Exp14 ``Basement~2'' of the
Hilti-Oxford dataset~\cite{zhang2023hilti}. Our contribution is not a new algorithm but a
controlled comparison: all four systems are bound to a common output contract (one
trajectory file per run, in the ground-truth frame, in TUM format), evaluated with identical
evo parameters, and each system is containerised so that its run can be reproduced. We
report trajectory accuracy (ATE), local drift (RPE) and, for the LiDAR-based systems, the
cloud-to-cloud distance between the reconstructed map and a terrestrial laser scan of the
same building. We also report what the benchmark itself can and cannot resolve: the
ground truth is accurate to 1--2\,cm, and at least one of the systems is not deterministic,
which together bound the differences that may be interpreted at all.

\paragraph*{MOLA-LO}
MOLA-LO~\cite{blanco2025mola} is a scan-to-model LiDAR odometry. Each incoming scan is
deskewed, down-sampled with a voxel filter and registered against a local voxel map with an
ICP-like optimiser that supports point-to-point and point-to-plane pairings and robust kernels.
Following KISS-ICP~\cite{vizzo2023kiss}, the pairing threshold adapts to the observed motion;
MOLA-LO extends this with a feedback controller that enlarges the search radius when the
alignment quality drops, e.g.\ when passing through doors. A constant-velocity model provides
the prior pose, and linear and angular velocities are estimated inside the ICP loop, so that
deskewing is refined during registration. The map is stored as a view-based map (keyframes with
raw scans), from which a dense point cloud is generated afterwards. We use the published
\texttt{hilti2022} profile without changes: the IMU frame is the vehicle frame, the
LiDAR--IMU extrinsics come from the dataset calibration, and the IMU is used for deskewing
and the initial pitch and roll. Loop closure is not part of the odometry pipeline.

\subsection{Dataset and Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All systems run on sequence Exp14 ``Basement~2'' of the Hilti-Oxford dataset~\cite{zhang2023hilti}:
a 73\,s handheld walk from a staircase through the basement of the Sheldonian Theatre,
including door openings. The platform carries a Hesai PandarXT-32 LiDAR (10\,Hz), five grayscale
fisheye cameras ($720\times540$, 40\,Hz) and a BMI085 IMU (400\,Hz). Exp14 is one of the
additional sequences with a dense 6-DoF ground truth (GT) in the IMU frame: 689 poses at about
10\,Hz over a 37.8\,m path, with gaps of up to 0.6\,s. The GT was obtained by registering each
LiDAR scan against a terrestrial laser scan of the building and is accurate to 1--2\,cm~\cite{zhang2023hilti}.

To make the systems comparable, every system writes one trajectory file per run in TUM format
($t\;x\;y\;z\;q_x\;q_y\;q_z\;q_w$, Unix time in seconds) that contains the pose of the IMU frame.
Systems that estimate the LiDAR pose are converted with $\mathbf{T}_{WI}=\mathbf{T}_{WL}\,\mathbf{T}_{LI}$
using the extrinsics from the dataset calibration.

All results reported here were produced on an AMD Ryzen~AI~9~365 (10~cores, 20~threads,
22\,GB RAM) under Ubuntu~24.04.4~LTS with ROS~2 Jazzy, and all trajectory metrics with
evo~1.31.1 (the version in the shared evaluation image). MOLA-LO is the binary release \texttt{ros-jazzy-mola-lidar-odometry}~3.3.0
together with \texttt{mp2p\_icp}~3.0.0 and \texttt{mola\_state\_estimation}~3.0.2, driven by
the published \texttt{hilti2022} profile; one pass over the 740 scans takes 35--46\,s, about
47--62\,ms per scan including bag decoding, so the system runs well within real time on this
machine. MOLA-LO parallelises its registration stage and is therefore not bit-reproducible:
over five runs of Exp14 the per-pose positions differ by 41\,mm on average (95th percentile
99\,mm, maximum 127\,mm), while the aggregated ATE RMSE stays inside a 3.5\,mm band
($0.0986\pm0.0013$\,m). The comparison below uses the first run; that band bounds how much
this choice matters. \todo{Felix, Merle, Rainhard: hardware and software versions of your

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
All trajectory metrics are computed with evo~\cite{grupp2017evo} using identical parameters.
Estimated and GT poses are associated by timestamp with a maximum difference of 50\,ms, and
each estimate is aligned to the GT with an SE(3) Umeyama alignment~\cite{umeyama1991} without
scale. The absolute trajectory error (ATE)~\cite{sturm2012tum} is the translational distance
between aligned and GT positions; we report RMSE, mean, median, maximum and standard deviation.
The relative pose error (RPE)~\cite{sturm2012tum} measures local drift over pose pairs that are
1\,m apart along the path, separately for translation and rotation. The 1\,m distance replaces
the 100--800\,m segments of the KITTI benchmark, which do not fit a 37.8\,m path. We also report
completeness, i.e.\ the share of GT timestamps with an associated estimate.

For the dense LiDAR-based maps, each map is transformed with the same Umeyama alignment as its
trajectory and compared to the terrestrial laser scan with a cloud-to-cloud distance in
CloudCompare. No additional ICP refinement is applied, so global drift remains visible in the
map error. OpenMAVIS produces only sparse feature points and is assessed qualitatively.

\section{Discussion}
\todo{Critical interpretation of system behaviour in the context of theory, differences between modalities.}

Three properties of the benchmark limit the interpretation. First, the dense GT is accurate to
only 1--2\,cm, so ATE differences below about 2\,cm do not support a ranking; the dataset authors
recommend the sparse millimetre-accurate control points once errors approach 1\,cm~\cite{zhang2023hilti}.
Second, the GT itself was produced by LiDAR registration against the laser scan, which may favour
LiDAR-based systems over the visual-inertial one. Third, a single 73\,s sequence has few
revisits, so loop closure hardly contributes and the results say little about generalisation.

Our own runs add three observations that constrain how the numbers may be read. First,
MOLA-LO is not bit-reproducible (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}): single poses move by up to 127\,mm
between runs, which is of the same order as the ATE itself, even though the aggregate RMSE
varies by less than 4\,mm. Run-to-run scatter and ground-truth accuracy together put the
resolution at which systems can be ranked at roughly 5\,cm, not at the millimetre precision
the metrics are printed with. Second, ground truth and estimate are both sampled at 10\,Hz
but in different phase, so the nearest-neighbour time difference is 50\,ms for every single
pose --- exactly the association threshold. Association therefore collapses to 64\,\% (439
of 689 poses) although the clocks do agree: fitting a continuous time lag, by interpolating
the ground-truth orientations onto the estimate timestamps with SLERP and minimising the
rotation error, puts the optimum at 0\,ms. A constant offset of $-50$\,ms restores 680
associations and shifts the ATE RMSE by 6\,mm. Any benchmark that associates two regularly
sampled trajectories with a threshold equal to half the sampling period is exposed to this
artefact, and the drop in completeness is easily misread as a failure of the estimator.
Third, a 1\,m RPE baseline is short enough that the rotation error dominates the
translational component: with a mean rotation error of $2.4^\circ$, a 1\,m segment
already accumulates $1\,\text{m}\cdot\sin 2.4^\circ \approx 4.2$\,cm. The 1\,m translational
RPE of 0.070\,m is therefore not a drift rate; the same trajectory yields 2.0\,\%, 1.6\,\%
and 1.4\,\% at baselines of 5, 10 and 20\,m. We report the 1\,m value for comparability
across the four systems, but read drift off the longer baselines.
